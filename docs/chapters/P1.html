2. Planning and investment programming

2.1 The question this process answers

Which programs and projects may ask for money in the budget, and who decided that they are priorities?

2.2 The idea in plain words

The budget covers one year. A road, a school program or an information system takes several years. The government therefore keeps plans for several years, and the budget of each year is meant to pay for one slice of those plans.

The plans form a chain. A development plan states goals for six years. An investment program lists the programs and projects that serve those goals, with their cost per year. Large projects are appraised one by one before they may be funded. Only then does a project appear in a budget proposal.

A case where the chain works: an agency lists a project in the investment program, obtains the required approvals, and proposes it in the budget as a new project (Tier 2, see chapter P0). The budget examiner can see that the project was planned and approved.

A case where the chain fails: an item enters the budget during legislation and was never in the investment program. The PFM Reforms Roadmap reports this as a gap: additions by Congress without regional consultation, ceilings that arrive late, and no single system that follows a proposal from plan to budget [ROADMAP2024].

Terms used in this chapter. The Philippine Development Plan (PDP) is the medium-term plan, now for 2023 to 2028. The Public Investment Program (PIP) is the list of priority programs and projects of all agencies for the same period. The Three-Year Rolling Infrastructure Program (TRIP) is the infrastructure part of that list for three years, extended by one year each year. PIPOL is the DEPDev web system in which agencies enter these programs and projects.

2.8 Timing

The call for the investment program is issued in the last quarter of the year, about fourteen months before the budget year starts. For the FY 2026 budget, agencies entered their programs and projects in PIPOL from 8 November to 6 December 2024 [PIP-MEMO]. The budget proposal followed in the first months of 2025. After the budget is passed, agencies update the yearly costs in PIPOL.

2.9 Variations and exceptions

  • Projects in information and communications technology need a separate clearance. The Budget Call names an information systems strategic plan and a steering committee for these projects [NBM156].
  • Projects funded by a grant are reviewed by DEPDev [ICC-NEWS].
  • A partnership project with a private firm follows the Public-Private Partnership Code. A national project of PHP 15 billion or more needs the approval of the Council on the recommendation of the ICC. A smaller project needs the ICC when it requires money of the budget law [RA11966 s.7]. See section 4.
  • Local governments have their own chain of plans. The local finance committee derives the spending ceiling from the approved local development plan, and at least 20 percent of the National Tax Allotment must go to development projects [LGC s.316, s.287]. See chapter E3, section 4.

2.10 Where the process fails in practice

Reported weakness Basis
PIPOL and OSBPS are separate systems. A working group of DEPDev and DBM was created to link them. No integrated system follows a proposal through its life ROADMAP2024
No standard project identifier exists, so a proposal is difficult to trace from planning to budgeting. The planning and the budgeting platforms lack interoperability. Many proposals and reports are still submitted on paper or in spreadsheet files SFA1
Budget ceilings reach agencies late in the planning period ROADMAP2024
Items are added during legislation without regional consultation ROADMAP2024
Project data are poorly integrated with fiscal planning and budget execution systems SFA6

3. The fiscal framework

3.1 The question this process answers

How large may the budget be in total, and who decides this before any agency writes a proposal?

3.2 The idea in plain words

The total of the budget is not the sum of what agencies ask for. It is decided first, from the top, by one committee. Agencies then receive a share of that total.

The decision follows from three numbers that depend on each other. If the government expects a certain revenue and accepts a certain deficit, the total it may spend is fixed: spending = revenue + deficit. The deficit must be financed, so the amount to borrow follows: borrowing = deficit + repayment of old debt that matures in the year, adjusted for any change in the cash balance.

A case where this works: revenue is collected as forecast, and agencies receive cash authority for all their allotments.

A case where this fails: revenue is lower than forecast. The total of allotments was already fixed by the budget law, but the cash is not there. The government must then borrow more, or DBM must slow the issue of cash authority (see chapter E5, section 3). This is why an agency can hold an allotment and still be unable to pay.

Terms used in this chapter. The Development Budget Coordination Committee (DBCC) is the committee that recommends these numbers. The fiscal program is the set of numbers for one year: revenue, disbursements, deficit and financing. The Executive Technical Board is the group of senior officials that prepares the work of the DBCC.

3.3 Authorities and their roles

Secretary of Finance Co-chairperson of the DBCC since Administrative Order No. 37 of 13 August 2025 (with effect from 27 April 2025). DOF brings the revenue forecast AO37

History of the DBCC, as stated on the DBM page: it was created on 14 May 1970 by Executive Order No. 232 as the Presidential Development Budget Committee, was renamed in 1972, and has its present composition under Executive Order No. 292 of 1987 [DBCC-PAGE].

The rule in that order, the Administrative Code, is its section on budget levels. The budget department, the finance department, the planning authority and the central bank recommend to the President, within the committee, the total levels of revenue, expenditure and debt. The section also states two limits. "The ordinary income of government shall be used primarily to provide appropriations for current operations." And no appropriation may be proposed "unless the amount involved is covered by the ordinary income, or unless it is supported by a proposal creating additional sources of funds or revenue, including those generated from domestic and foreign borrowings, sufficient to cover the same" [EO292-B6 s.13]. I read this section through a summary with quotations.

3.6 Information systems

No single system is named in the sources for the work of the DBCC. The inputs come from the systems of each member: collection data from BIR, BOC and the treasury systems (chapter E5, section 4), debt data from BTr (chapter E5, section 3), and spending data from the reports of agencies in URS (chapter A4).

3.7 Controls: what each one checks, and what it does not check

Control What it checks What it does not check
Ceiling in the Budget Call That the sum of proposals stays inside the spending program Whether each proposal is worth its cost
Rule that Congress may not increase the President's proposed appropriations [CONST VI.25(1)] That the total proposed by the President is an upper limit for the items specified in the budget The distribution between items. Unprogrammed appropriations and automatic appropriations follow other rules (chapter L5)
Allotment Release Program That releases in the year stay inside the fiscal program The timing of cash
Cash authority (NCA) That payments in a period stay inside the cash plan Whether obligations were already incurred

3.8 Timing

The DBCC decides the numbers of a budget year before the Budget Call, which DBM issued in January for FY 2027 [NBM156]. It reviews the numbers again during the year (GK). The numbers of the current year are compared with actual results each month in the Cash Operations Report [NBC599].

3.9 Variations and exceptions

  • The DBCC also recommends the consolidated public sector financial position, which includes government corporations and local governments [DBCC-PAGE]. Chapter A6, section 2, shows the rows of this table.
  • The DBCC issues a Fiscal Risks Statement on events that would make revenue, spending or debt differ from the program. The latest statement that I found is that of 2023 [FRS2023]. See section 4.
  • A reduction of the share of local governments in national taxes is possible only when the national government has an unmanageable public sector deficit, on the recommendation of the Secretaries of Finance, of the Interior and Local Government, and of Budget and Management, and never below 30 percent [LGC s.284].

3.10 Where the process fails in practice

Reported weakness Basis
The composition of actual spending differed from the approved budget by more than 15 percent in each of the three years assessed (PEFA indicator 2.1, score D). The assessment measured this by administrative classification PEFA2025
The current debt strategy of BTr does not meet the PEFA requirements for a medium-term debt strategy (PEFA indicator 13.3, score D). The recording and the approval of debt are rated A (indicators 13.1 and 13.2) PEFA2025
Cash forecasting is rated A (PEFA indicator 21.2): a cash flow committee makes a monthly forecast and updates it with the actual flows. Commitment controls are rated B (indicator 25.2): they limit commitments to the projected cash, but the assessment adds that cash availability is not always reliable PEFA2025

4. Fiscal risks, public-private partnerships and contingent liabilities

4.1 The question this process answers

The budget law lists the spending that is decided for one year. Which payments may become necessary although no budget line of today names them, who monitors each source of such payments, and from which authority would they be paid?

4.2 The idea in plain words

A fiscal risk is an event that would make revenue, spending or debt differ from the plan. Some risks are general, for example slower growth of the economy. Others come from promises that the government has already made.

A contingent liability is such a promise: the government will pay IF a stated event occurs. Example: the national government guarantees a loan of a government corporation. If the corporation repays the loan, the government pays nothing. If the corporation cannot repay, the government must pay the lender.

The difficulty for the budget process is the date. On the day of the promise no money leaves the Treasury, so no appropriation, no obligation and no payment is recorded. The promise does not appear in any record of chapters E0 to E6 or of chapter A4. It appears only in two places: in a statement of risks, and later, if the event occurs, as a payment for which an authority must then exist.

The accounting rules say the same for the books. The Manual defines a contingent liability as "A possible obligation that arises from past events, and whose existence will be confirmed only by the occurrence or non-occurrence of one or more uncertain future events not wholly within the control of the entity", or a present obligation for which a payment is not probable or cannot be measured [GAM-V1 Ch.18 s.2]. Its rule is: "An entity shall not recognize a contingent liability. However, it is disclosed unless the possibility of an outflow of resources" is remote [GAM-V1 Ch.18 s.3]. A guarantee that is not yet called is therefore named in the notes to the financial statements and is not a liability in the statement of financial position. It becomes a recorded liability, called a provision, when a payment is probable and the amount can be estimated [GAM-V1 Ch.18 s.3].

The law has one authority ready for the day on which a guarantee is called: "national government guarantees of obligations which are drawn upon" are among the automatic appropriations [EO292-B6 s.26].

A public-private partnership (PPP) produces both kinds of future payment. A private firm pays for the construction of a road or a hospital and operates it. The government pays later in instalments, or promises support under stated conditions. The decision is made once, at the signing of the contract, and it binds the budgets of many later years.

For such a contract the Manual has a rule that differs from the rule for a guarantee. The government body that grants the concession records the asset that the private operator builds, when it "controls or regulates what services the operator must provide with the asset, to whom it must provide them, and at what price" and controls the remaining interest in the asset at the end of the term [GAM-V1 Ch.14 s.3]. With the asset it records a liability of the same amount: a financial liability when the government must pay the operator, or the grant of a right when the operator earns from the users [GAM-V1 Ch.14 s.5]. A partnership of this kind is therefore in the books from the start, and a guarantee is not.

A case where the control works: before a partnership contract is signed, a body above the agency approves the project, and the Department of Finance clears each promise of the national government. The yearly payments are then included in each budget proposal, and a fund exists for the promises that become due.

A case where it fails: a promise is given outside these procedures, for example a guarantee in the charter of a corporation or a letter of support, and nobody records it. It appears for the first time when the creditor asks for payment. The budget of that year has no line for it.

Terms. An availability payment is a regular payment by the government to the private partner for keeping the facility available, whether or not the public uses it. A government undertaking is any contribution or support that the government gives to the private partner, for example a subsidy or a guarantee. Viability gap funding is a subsidy that makes a project financially possible for the private partner when the income from users is too low. Net lending is the amount that the national government lends to government corporations, less repayments.
